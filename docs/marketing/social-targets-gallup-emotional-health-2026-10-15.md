## What the report actually says, in one paragraph

Across 97 countries, 81% of adults used social media in the past week. After adjusting for who uses it, the people in the middle band (30 minutes to 2 hours a day) report the best mix of daily emotions. Heavy users (2h+) report slightly more anger, loneliness, sadness, stress and worry. Light or non-users report slightly less enjoyment and slightly lower life ratings. More time online does *not* mean steadily worse wellbeing, but in high-income countries like the US, heavy use goes with lower life ratings (about 0.3 points on a 0 to 10 scale). Separately, worry (38%) and stress (36%) are the most common daily emotions worldwide, and stress is up 7 points since 2006. All findings are associations, and the effects are small.

### G1 · The goal isn't zero (long-form, research slot)

> The goal isn't zero screen time.
>
> It's a smaller number you can actually hold.
>
> Gallup just asked adults in 97 countries two questions: did you use social media this week, and about how long do you spend on it a day? Then it compared the answers with how people said their days went.
>
> The people who did best weren't the ones who'd quit. After accounting for age, income, health and the rest, adults who used social media for 30 minutes to two hours a day reported a better mix of daily emotions than both groups on either side of them. People over two hours were slightly more likely to report stress, worry, sadness, anger and loneliness. People under 30 minutes were slightly less likely to report enjoyment, and they rated their lives a little lower.
>
> The differences are small, and Gallup is clear that this shows a link, not a cause. But it does undercut the most common version of this goal: "I'm quitting social media." That's the version that tends to end in a week.
>
> A commitment that holds looks different:
>
> 1. A number, not a vow. "Under two hours a day" is checkable. "Less phone" isn't.
> 2. Proof you don't have to remember. Your phone already keeps a screen-time report. Screenshot it once a week.
> 3. Someone who sees it. A number only you look at is easy to renegotiate on a Sunday night.
>
> Cutting back is a fine goal. It just needs the same things any other commitment needs: a measure, a record and a date when someone asks how it went.
>
> If you've tried to cut back on social media before, what made it slide back up?
>
> #Accountability #FollowThrough

### G2 · Time online isn't someone to count on (long-form, angle only)

Hook: "Two hours a day online is not the same as having someone to count on." Point: Gallup measures social media use and social support (having someone to count on for help) separately. Heavy use is the pattern linked to slightly more loneliness. A recurring group, with names, a date and commitments, is a different kind of connection than a feed. **Guardrails:** no claim that groups or AG reduce loneliness, no raw user/nonuser loneliness numbers, and no "social media is bad" framing. Needs a careful draft and a Grant read before carding.
