## Instructions for Claude (read this first)

When writing Field notes articles, marketing copy, social posts, or product copy for Accountability Guild, use this file as the citation source of record.

**Rules:**

1. **Only cite ✓ entries in anything published.** ⚠ = plausible but unverified (verify first). ✗ = do not cite ever (citation broken or paper likely nonexistent).
2. **Cite by author-year with a DOI link**, e.g. `[Harkin et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)`. Never link ResearchGate, SciSpace, Scilit, or Semantic Scholar in published content.
3. **Use the exact stats given here** — don't round d=0.34 to "about a third" of anything, don't inflate "medium effect" to "dramatic." If a stat isn't in this file, don't invent it; check the paper.
4. **Never use ego-depletion / "willpower is a muscle" framing** (contested science — see Caveats). "People who succeed avoid temptation rather than resist it" is the safe version.
5. **Effect-size translation for lay audiences:** d≈0.2 "small", d≈0.5 "moderate", d≈0.8+ "large". A d=0.40 can honestly be called "a meaningful, causal boost"; it cannot be called "transformative."
6. **Domain honesty:** most habit/open-goal evidence comes from physical-activity research. For business/writing/finance commitments say "research on health behaviors suggests..." rather than implying direct evidence.
7. **The strongest AG-relevant claim chain** (all ✓, use freely): goals work best when public + group-framed + difficult (Epton 2017) → progress monitoring causally drives attainment, most when publicly reported + physically recorded (Harkin 2016) → financial commitment devices align present actions with long-term goals (Rogers 2014) → accountability sustains when supportive, collaborative, and trust-based rather than coercive (Mohr 2011; Riddell 2022).

**Format templates by channel:**

- *Field notes (blog):* "A meta-analysis of 138 experiments (N≈20,000) found that prompting people to monitor progress increased goal attainment (d = 0.40) — with the biggest gains when progress was reported publicly and physically recorded ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025))."
- *Marketing site:* short claim + link to a research/methodology page, not inline academic citations. "Public progress tracking measurably increases follow-through. It's the most-replicated finding in goal science."
- *Social:* one stat, one hook, no hedging beyond accuracy. "The '21 days to form a habit' thing? A 2024 meta-analysis put the real median at 59–66 days. Your accountability group needs to outlast month one."

## Part 1: Goal-setting and motivation

### 1. Unique Effects of Setting Goals on Behavior Change ✓
**Epton, Currie & Armitage, 2017** — *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 85(12) — [10.1037/ccp0000260](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000260)

- 141 papers, 384 effect sizes, N=16,523 — RCTs only, isolating the *unique* effect of goal setting from other techniques. Small-to-medium positive effect across behaviors: d=0.34 (95% CI [0.28, 0.41]).
- Strongest moderators: goal setting worked significantly better when the goal was (a) **difficult**, (b) **set publicly**, and (c) **a group goal**.
- Weaker (suggestive) evidence for: external monitoring of the behavior by others without feedback, and face-to-face delivery.
- Goal origin didn't matter: self-set, collaboratively set, and assigned goals worked about equally well. What matters is calibrating difficulty to ability.
- Interesting angle: the authors propose goal setting as "a fundamental component of successful interventions" — it works across cognitive, sport, health, and educational domains.
- **AG angle:** the three strongest moderators — difficult, public, group — are all structural features of an AG group. Solo apps deliver none of them.

### 2. Multi-Component Goal Setting Interventions for Physical Activity ✓
**McEwan et al., 2016** — *Health Psychology Review* 10(1):67–88 — [10.1080/17437199.2015.1104258](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2015.1104258)

- Screened 41,038 articles → 45 included (52 interventions, 126 effect sizes, N=5,912). Medium positive effect on physical activity: d=0.552 (95% CI [0.43, 0.67], Z=9.03).
- Specific goals (d=0.59) were NOT significantly better than vague/non-specific goals (d=0.51) — direct challenge to SMART orthodoxy.
- Moderator analyses ran across 20 variables spanning study features, samples, goal content, and added behavior-change techniques.
- **AG angle:** supports letting members write commitments in their own words rather than forcing a rigid format.

### 3. Defining Open Goals for the Promotion of Health Behaviours ✓
**Swann, Goddard, Schweickle, Hawkins, Williamson et al., 2025** — *Health Psychology Review* — [10.1080/17437199.2025.2467695](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2025.2467695)

- First formal definition of open goals: "nonspecific and phrased in an exploratory way, with **measurable parameters**, producing **graded outcomes**" (vs. binary pass/fail).
- Reviewed all 16 existing empirical studies on open goals (quality-assessed via MMAT); explicitly responds to field-wide calls to re-examine the assumption that goals must be specific to work.
- Key nuance for content: open ≠ vague. An open goal is still measurable ("see how many workouts I can log") — it just grades the outcome instead of pass/failing it.
- **AG angle:** a member's first cycle could use an open commitment ("log every workout and see where I land") before converting to a hard threshold in cycle two.

### 4. Goal Types in Active vs. Insufficiently Active Adults ✓
**Hawkins, Crust, Swann & Jackman, 2020** — *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* — [10.1016/j.psychsport.2020.101661](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2020.101661)

- 4 (goal condition) × 3 (attempt) × 2 (group) design; active (n=18) and insufficiently active (n=18) adults; 6-minute walk tests. Small N — treat as initial evidence, not definitive.
- Crossover interaction: insufficiently active people walked farther under open goals than SMART goals (p<.001); active people walked farther under SMART goals (p<.001).
- Same crossover for enjoyment/pleasure (p<.05): open goals felt better to the inactive; SMART goals felt better to the active.
- **AG angle:** goal-format guidance should branch by experience: veterans get specific thresholds, newcomers get exploratory commitments.

### 5. The Effect of Goal Setting on Group Performance ✓
**Kleingeld, van Mierlo & Arends, 2011** — *Journal of Applied Psychology* 96(6):1289–1304 — [10.1037/a0024315](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024315)

- 49 effect sizes. Specific difficult group goals vs. nonspecific: large effect, d=0.80. (Gemini's "N=75,811" not confirmed — don't quote a participant count.)
- The framing of *individual* goals inside a group is decisive: "egocentric" goals (maximize my own performance) in interdependent tasks: d=−1.75 — actively destructive. "Groupcentric" goals (maximize my contribution to the group): d=1.20.
- Authors' own conclusion: individual goals "should be used with caution in interdependent groups."
- **AG angle:** the single best evidence against leaderboard/competition mechanics and for "the group is counting on you" framing. Commitment templates should read as contributions, not contests.

### 6. The Development of Goal Setting Theory: A Half Century Retrospective ✓
**Locke & Latham, 2019** — *Motivation Science* 5(2) — [10.1037/mot0000127](https://doi.org/10.1037/mot0000127)

- The theory's authors summarizing 50+ years: built inductively from hundreds of studies, thousands of participants; has held up for decades across tasks, nationalities, settings, and levels (individual → organizational).
- Four mechanisms: goals direct attention, mobilize effort, sustain persistence, prompt strategy development. Five moderators: commitment, feedback, task complexity, ability, situational constraints.
- Newer findings they highlight: (a) set *learning* goals instead of performance goals when skills are lacking; (b) goal effects are enhanced by having people **write at length about their goals**; (c) even subconsciously primed goals affect job performance.
- **AG angle:** "write at length" supports AG's written-commitment + written-report cycle structure; feedback as a core moderator supports cycle-close reviews.

### 7. Performance and Psychological Effects of Goal Setting in Sport ✓
**Williamson, Swann, Bennett, Bird, Goddard, Schweickle & Jackman, 2022** — *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology* — [10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723](https://doi.org/10.1080/1750984X.2022.2116723)

- Screened 17,841 articles → 27 eligible. Effect on performance by goal type: **process d=1.36, performance d=0.44, outcome d=0.09**.
- Process goals also drive self-efficacy hard (d=1.11), via increased sense of personal control.
- Competitive goals (beat others) reduce engagement and increase performance anxiety.
- **AG angle:** the sharpest "commit to behaviors, not outcomes" evidence. "Ship 3 sales calls a day" (process) over "close 5 deals" (outcome) in goal-writing guidance.

### 8. Are SMART Goals Fit-for-Purpose? ✓
**Stewart, McMillan, Hu, Collins, El-Den, O'Reilly & Wheeler, 2024** — *International Journal for Quality in Health Care* 36(1) — [10.1093/intqhc/mzae009](https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzae009)

- 512 real goals co-designed by pharmacists and mental-health service users (PharMIbridge RCT), scored with the SMART-GEM evaluation tool.
- Fewer than 25% of goals specified how achievement would be measured. **93.5% had no progress-monitoring plan at all.**
- Shows the chasm between the SMART format as taught and goals as actually written in the real world — even by trained professionals in a structured program.
- **AG angle:** killer stat for the product's reason to exist: people don't naturally build measurement or monitoring into goals; AG's cycle structure supplies both. (Don't quote Gemini's "100% poor quality" — unconfirmed.)

### 9. Goal Motives and MCII Facilitate Strategic Persistence and Disengagement ✓
**Riddell, Sedikides, Gucciardi, Ben, Thøgersen-Ntoumani & Ntoumanis, 2022** — *Journal of Applied Social Psychology* — [10.1111/jasp.12915](https://doi.org/10.1111/jasp.12915)

- Three experiments on motives behind goal pursuit. Autonomous ("I want to") motives → adaptive appraisals, coping, and persistence on attainable goals. Controlled ("I have to") motives → threat appraisals and premature abandonment.
- MCII training buffers the damage of controlled motivation.
- Autonomous motives also predict *faster strategic quitting* of genuinely unattainable goals and re-engagement with alternatives — persistence and knowing-when-to-stop come from the same root.
- **AG angle:** members must author their own commitments (autonomy), even in owner-led groups. Also supports hall passes and mid-cycle commitment revision as features, not loopholes.

### 10. Nonspecific Goals in Physical Activity Promotion ✓
**Swann, Schweickle, Peoples, Goddard, Stevens & Vella, 2020** — *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* — [10.1080/10413200.2020.1815100](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2020.1815100)

- 82 healthy adults (59 women; mean age 48), randomized to open, do-your-best, as-well-as-possible, SMART, or no-goal control across 6-minute walk tests.
- All four goal types beat no-goal control on distance; **no significant performance differences among goal types** — the differences were psychological.
- Open goals uniquely produced higher interest in repeating the session AND in pursuing an ongoing program. Do-your-best goals demanded the most mental effort.
- **AG angle:** "any commitment beats no commitment" + "the format you choose determines whether you come back." Return intent is the metric that matters for recurring cycles.

## Part 2: Habit formation and automaticity

### 11. Time to Form a Habit ✓
**Singh, Murphy, Maher & Smith, 2024** — *Healthcare* 12(23):2488 — [10.3390/healthcare12232488](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488)

- 20 studies, 2,601 participants. Median time to peak automaticity: **59–66 days**; mean: 106–154 days; individual range: **4 to 335 days**.
- Directly kills the "21 days" myth (which traces to a 1960s plastic surgeon's observation, not research — useful color for articles).
- Stronger habit trajectories: morning execution, self-selected behaviors, and positive affect (enjoying the behavior).
- Habits consolidate gradually, not at a threshold — automaticity accrues with each repetition.
- **AG angle:** cadence math. A monthly group gets ~2 cycle-closes inside the median formation window; a weekly group gets 8–9. Argument for weekly/bi-weekly cadence for new-habit commitments, monthly for maintenance.

### 12. Effects of Habit and Intention on Behavior ⚠ (details unverified)
**2023** — *Motivation Science* — DOI given as 10.1037/mot0000294 (could not confirm via API; likely Hagger et al. per archived PDF)

- Claimed: 267 studies; habit and intention are independent, parallel predictors; habit partially mediates past→future behavior; associations strongest for low-complexity behaviors in stable contexts.
- Direction of findings is consistent with the verified literature (Gardner 2011, Rebar 2014/2016) — but verify the citation before quoting numbers from it.

### 13. Exercise Habit Formation in New Gym Members ✓
**Kaushal & Rhodes, 2015** — *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* — [10.1007/s10865-015-9640-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10865-015-9640-7)

- 111 new gym members tracked for 12 weeks — real-world onboarding population, directly analogous to new AG members.
- Habit threshold: **at least 4 exercise sessions/week for at least 6 weeks**.
- Habit trajectory predicted by consistency (β=0.21), low behavioral complexity (β=0.19), environment (β=0.17), positive affect (β=0.13).
- **AG angle:** commitment-sizing guidance — "4x/week for 6 weeks" is a concrete, citable template for a first commitment. Simple + enjoyable + consistent beats ambitious.

### 14. Self-Report Habit Index Meta-Analysis ✓
**Gardner, de Bruijn & Lally, 2011** — *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* — [10.1007/s12160-011-9286-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12160-011-9286-y)

- 21 datasets on nutrition and physical activity. Habit–behavior correlation: r=0.44–0.46 (moderate-to-strong).
- Habit strength weakens the intention–behavior link as automaticity rises: once something is habitual, "wanting to" stops being the driver.

### 15. The Use of 'Habit' in Health Behaviour Research ✓
**Gardner, 2015** — *Health Psychology Review* — [10.1080/17437199.2013.876238](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2013.876238)

- Definitional review (136 studies, 8 reviews): habit = a non-conscious context→response association (automaticity), NOT mere frequency. Doing something daily by force of will is not yet a habit.
- Cued associations run on an impulsive pathway; overriding them requires effortful top-down inhibition — which is exactly what's depleted on bad days.

### 16. Habits Predict Physical Activity on Days When Intentions Are Weak ✓
**Rebar, Elavsky, Maher, Doerksen & Conroy, 2014** — *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology* 36(2):157–165 — [10.1123/jsep.2013-0173](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.2013-0173)

- 128 university students; daily self-reported intentions + accelerometers for 14 days (objective measurement, not just self-report).
- On days when someone's intentions were weaker than *their own typical level*, habit strength predicted activity. On typical/strong-intention days, habit didn't add prediction.
- The elegant framing: habits are a floor, not a ceiling. They don't make good days better; they stop bad days from going to zero.
- **AG angle:** great narrative stat for "the group gets you through the weeks you don't feel like it" — external structure serves the same floor function while habits are still forming.

### 17. Non-Conscious Regulatory Processes in Physical Activity ✓
**Rebar et al., 2016** — *Health Psychology Review* — [10.1080/17437199.2015.1130609](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2015.1130609)

- Systematic review of ~52 studies on implicit processes (habit, automatic associations, priming).
- Habit strength predicts behavior even after controlling for conscious motivation and planning.
- Argues interventions should target cue-contingent automaticity because purely motivational programs suffer high attrition.

### 18. Action Plans, Habit, and Intention Strength (Web-Based Intervention) ✓
**Rebar, Williams, Short, Plotnikoff, Duncan, Mummery et al., 2023** — *Psychology & Health* — [10.1080/08870446.2023.2241777](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2023.2241777) *(title corrected: "The impact of action plans on habit and intention strength for physical activity...: is it the thought that counts?")*

- N=115 (68.7% female, mean age 44) in a 3-month web intervention; up to 24 action plans possible per person.
- **Consistency** of action planning (same how/where/when/with whom) predicted physical activity at 9 months and intention + habit strength at 3 and 9 months.
- Surprise finding: **frequency** of action planning was *negatively* associated with intention strength — churning out many different plans backfires.
- Authors' advice: "avoid encouraging making many distinct action plans; encourage stable contexts through consistent action planning."
- **AG angle:** carry commitments forward across cycles by default ("same commitment, next cycle") rather than prompting members to write fresh ones each time.

### 19. General and Habit-Specific Self-Efficacy in Habit Building ✓
**2021** — *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:643753 — [10.3389/fpsyg.2021.643753](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.643753)

- University students tracked 6 weeks. Positive feedback loop: habit-specific self-efficacy → future automaticity → higher subsequent self-efficacy.
- Higher habit-specific self-efficacy reduces motivational interference (felt pull of temptations) during execution.
- **AG angle:** logging proof isn't just accountability — visible streaks of wins feed the self-efficacy loop. Surface past-cycle successes at cycle start.

## Part 3: Monitoring, accountability, and self-regulation

### 21. Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? ✓
**Harkin, Webb, Chang, Prestwich, Conner, Kellar, Benn & Sheeran, 2016** — *Psychological Bulletin* — [10.1037/bul0000025](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)

- 138 randomized experiments, N=19,951 — the definitive causal test, not correlational.
- Prompting progress monitoring increases monitoring itself (d=1.98 — enormous; people mostly don't track unless structured to) and increases goal attainment (d=0.40, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48]).
- Change in monitoring frequency *mediates* the attainment effect: the tracking is the mechanism, not a side effect.
- Effects significantly larger when progress is **reported publicly** and when it is **physically recorded**.
- **AG angle:** this is AG's scientific cornerstone. The product operationalizes both amplifiers simultaneously: logged proof (physical recording) + group visibility (public reporting). Lead with this study everywhere.

### 22. Supportive Accountability ✓
**Mohr, Cuijpers & Lehman, 2011** — *Journal of Medical Internet Research* 13(1):e30 — [10.2196/jmir.1602](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602)

- The theoretical model for why human support makes digital interventions stick. Drawn from organizational psychology, motivation theory, and computer-mediated communication research.
- Adherence rises through accountability to a person perceived as **trustworthy, benevolent, and having expertise** (legitimacy, not surveillance).
- Expectations must be clear, **process-oriented**, and **determined with the user's involvement**. Reciprocity — the user visibly benefiting from the relationship — should be explicit.
- Accountability's effect is moderated by intrinsic motivation (highly motivated users need less support) and mediated by communication medium (phone, IM, email each have trade-offs).
- **AG angle:** the design spec for both modes. Owner-led = the coach's expertise/benevolence carries it; peer = mutual reciprocity carries it. Cycle expectations are member-authored (involvement) and process-based (log workouts, not lose weight). Every component maps.

### 23. Everyday Temptations ✓
**Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs, 2012** — *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 102(6):1318–1335 — [10.1037/a0026545](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026545)

- Experience sampling: 205 adults, 7,827 desire reports across one week — a map of real-world temptation.
- People spend an enormous share of waking life desiring things that conflict with their goals; attempting resistance drops enactment of conflicted desires from ~70% to ~17%.
- People with high trait self-control succeed mainly by **structuring their lives to avoid temptation**, not by heroic in-the-moment resistance.
- (Same-day depletion finding is ego-depletion-adjacent — don't build claims on it; see Caveats.)
- **AG angle:** "disciplined people aren't resisting more, they're arranging their environment better" — and an accountability group is environment design for follow-through.

### 24. Mind-Sets Matter ✓
**Burnette, O'Boyle, VanEpps, Pollack & Finkel, 2013** — *Psychological Bulletin* — [10.1037/a0029531](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029531)

- 113 studies, N=28,217. Growth mindset predicts learning goals (r=0.187), mastery strategies (r=0.227), fewer helpless strategies (r=−0.238), fewer negative emotions (r=−0.233). Real but small effects — don't oversell.
- Key moderator: growth-mindset benefits are **amplified under ego threat and failure feedback** — they matter most right after a miss.
- **AG angle:** informs post-miss copy at cycle close: frame the miss as data for next cycle's adjustment ("what will you change?"), not as a verdict.

### 25. Self-Regulation in Childhood as a Predictor of Future Outcomes ✓
**Robson, Allen & Howard, 2020** — *Psychological Bulletin* 146(4):324–354 — [10.1037/bul0000227](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000227)

- 150 studies, 745 effect sizes, N=215,212; evidence across 25 discrete outcomes.
- Childhood self-regulation predicts later academic performance, social competence, employment, physical/mental health, and lower substance abuse into adulthood.
- Background/authority stat — establishes self-regulation as a life-outcome variable worth investing in. Not a direct product claim.

### 26. Executive Functions ✓
**Diamond, 2013** — *Annual Review of Psychology* 64:135–168 — [10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750)

- Canonical review. Three core executive functions: inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility — all effortful, top-down, prefrontal.
- **Stress, sleep deprivation, loneliness, and physical inactivity** each demonstrably impair executive function, pushing behavior back onto automatic loops.
- Notable detail: loneliness is on that list — social connection isn't just motivational fluff; isolation measurably degrades the cognitive machinery of self-control.
- **AG angle:** "your group is cognitive infrastructure" — an evidence-flavored angle connecting AG's social layer to executive function.

### 27. Self-Monitoring in Weight Loss ✓
**Burke, Wang & Sevick, 2011** — *Journal of the American Dietetic Association* 111(1):92–102 — [10.1016/j.jada.2010.10.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jada.2010.10.008)

- 22 studies across dietary, exercise, and self-weighing monitoring. Consistent, significant association between **monitoring consistency** and weight-loss success — consistency of tracking, not perfection of behavior, is the correlate.
- Digital tools flagged (in 2011) as the promising route to better recording compliance.

### 28. Commitment Devices ✓
**Rogers, Milkman & Volpp, 2014** — *JAMA* 311(20):2065–2066 — [10.1001/jama.2014.3485](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.3485)

- JAMA viewpoint defining commitment devices: voluntarily imposed constraints/penalties (deposit contracts, social stakes) aligning present actions with long-term goals. Inexpensive and effective.
- The catch: **low voluntary uptake**, driven by present bias and overoptimism about future willpower — people who most need them least choose them.
- (Gemini's "40% increase in smoking cessation" unverified — the underlying deposit-contract RCT exists (Giné, Karlan & Zinman) but verify numbers there before quoting.)
- **AG angle:** AG solves the uptake problem socially — you adopt the commitment device by joining a group, at a moment of high motivation, with the group normalizing it. Forfeits-to-kitty (not to the company) keeps the device feeling like self-binding rather than a vendor fee.

### 29. Mental Contrasting With Implementation Intentions: Meta-Analysis ✓
**Wang, Wang & Gai, 2021** — *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:565202 — [10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202)

- 21 studies, 24 effect sizes, N=15,907. MCII (visualize the wish → contrast with the obstacle → plan an if-then response) improves goal attainment: g=0.336. Authors note likely publication-bias inflation.
- Delivery matters: interactive person-led training (g=0.465) beats handing people a document (g=0.277) — human interaction nearly doubles the effect.
- **AG angle:** a cycle-start prompt worth testing: "what's most likely to derail this commitment, and what's your if-then plan?" Also: the interactive>document finding is a mini-argument for live cycle-close meetings.

## Synthesis: what the literature converges on

**1. Willpower doesn't scale; structure does.** Executive functions are effortful and degrade under stress, poor sleep, loneliness, and inactivity (Diamond 2013). People with strong self-control mostly avoid temptation rather than resist it (Hofmann 2012). Durable change comes from offloading behavior to context-cued habits, which act as a floor under motivation dips (Rebar 2014).

**2. Goal type must match the person and the phase.** Difficult, public, group-framed goals maximize the core effect (Epton 2017). Process goals crush outcome goals for performance (d=1.36 vs 0.09; Williamson 2022). For newcomers, open/exploratory goals beat SMART psychologically — more enjoyment, more return intent (Hawkins 2020; Swann 2020; open-goals review 2025). SMART earns its keep only for the already-practiced. In the wild, people write goals with no measurement or monitoring plan anyway (Stewart 2024).

**3. Monitoring is the engine; publicity and recording are the multipliers.** Progress monitoring causally drives attainment (d=0.40), most when publicly reported and physically recorded (Harkin 2016). People barely monitor without structure (monitoring uplift d=1.98). Tracking consistency, not behavioral perfection, correlates with success (Burke 2011).

**4. Accountability works when supportive, backfires when coercive.** Trust, benevolence, expertise, co-designed process expectations, explicit reciprocity (Mohr 2011). Controlled "have-to" motives trigger threat and abandonment; autonomous "want-to" motives sustain persistence — and enable smart quitting (Riddell 2022). Commitment devices work but must be voluntarily adopted (Rogers 2014).

**5. Habits take months, not weeks.** Median ~2 months, mean 3.5–5 months, range 4–335 days (Singh 2024). ~4x/week for 6+ weeks establishes an exercise habit (Kaushal & Rhodes 2015). Consistency of context and plan beats variety and volume of planning (Rebar 2023). Enjoyment, simplicity, and morning timing accelerate.

**6. Group framing amplifies — or destroys.** Group goals d=0.80; contribution-framed individual goals d=1.20; me-first goals in interdependent groups d=−1.75 (Kleingeld 2011). Competitive framing raises anxiety and lowers engagement (Williamson 2022).

## Implications for Accountability Guild

- **The core loop is the most evidence-backed configuration available.** Public commitments + logged proof + cycle reports = public reporting + physical recording (Harkin 2016), delivered as difficult/public/group goals (Epton 2017). This chain is AG's strongest defensible marketing claim.
- **Forfeits are commitment devices done right.** Voluntary adoption at join-time solves the uptake problem Rogers (2014) identifies; kitty-pooling keeps it self-binding rather than punitive.
- **Peer and owner-led modes both map onto Supportive Accountability** (Mohr 2011): expertise carries owner-led groups, reciprocity carries peer groups; both require member-authored, process-oriented commitments.
- **Goal-writing guidance:** teach process commitments ("log 4 workouts") over outcome commitments ("lose 5 lbs") — d=1.36 vs 0.09. Offer open commitments for first-cycle members. Never force SMART.
- **Default to carrying commitments forward.** Consistency of planning beats fresh planning every cycle (Rebar 2023) — "same commitment, next cycle" should be the one-click default.
- **Cadence content:** habits median ~2 months to form (Singh 2024) → weekly/bi-weekly cycles for formation, monthly for maintenance. "4x/week for 6 weeks" is a citable starter template (Kaushal & Rhodes 2015).
- **Anti-leaderboard stance is evidence-based** (Kleingeld 2011; Williamson 2022). Watch kitty policies that reward "top performers" — contribution framing, not competition framing.
- **Post-miss UX:** frame misses as data + next-cycle adjustment (Burnette 2013 — mindset effects peak at failure; Riddell 2022 — autonomy enables healthy re-engagement). Hall passes are theory-consistent.
- **Content whitespace tie-in:** articles 8–13 (group + money-stakes + pooled kitty) can be underpinned by this exact chain — no competitor found publishing evidence-based content on deposit contracts + supportive accountability + group goals as one system.

## Caveats

- **Ego depletion is contested.** Large registered replications find effects near zero. Never use "willpower is a depletable muscle" framing. Safe alternative: avoidance-over-resistance (Hofmann 2012) and habit-as-floor (Rebar 2014).
- **Publication bias:** Wang (2021) self-reports it; assume meta-analytic effects are upper bounds generally.
- **Domain skew:** habit and open-goal evidence is mostly physical activity. Hedge when generalizing to work/finance/creative commitments.
- **Mindset effects are small** (r≈0.15–0.24). Supporting seasoning, not a headline.
- **Small-N studies:** Hawkins 2020 (n=36 total) and Swann 2020 (n=82) are initial evidence — pair them with the meta-analyses rather than citing alone.
