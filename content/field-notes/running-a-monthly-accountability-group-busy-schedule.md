# Running a Monthly Accountability Group on a Busy Schedule

*By Grant Tilus*

**Quick summary.** A monthly accountability group works on a busy schedule if members record their progress as they go and agree in advance on what counts as a legitimate excuse. Research on health behaviors suggests a shorter rhythm suits a brand-new habit, so monthly fits maintenance and genuinely full calendars best.

The month gets busy. The date for the group meeting sneaks up, and the goal you promised to work on is exactly where you left it…with limited progress. Anyone who has tried to keep a commitment to an accountability group alive on a full calendar knows the feeling, and it is the reason some people assume a monthly group cannot work; at least until your in a groove.

We built Accountability Guild for our own accountability group, which meets on the first Thursday of every month. A monthly rhythm suited us as busy people with work and families, but it only worked if the group built in a few habits that a weekly group gets for free. This guide covers what those habits are, when monthly is the right choice, and how to set one up.

## A monthly rhythm works for busy people, with two conditions

Yes, with conditions. A monthly window gives each commitment room to fit around a full calendar, and an agreed excuse for real emergencies absorbs a genuinely bad month without breaking the group's trust. The conditions are that you record progress asynchronous as you go and that monthly suits the commitment you are actually making.

The common mistake is choosing monthly because it feels easier, then treating the longer window as permission to stop paying attention until the last few days. Monthly is a real choice, not a fallback, but it asks more of the group's habits. [Accountability Guild](/how-it-works) treats weekly, bi-weekly, and monthly as equally valid ways to run a group, and the group or its owner picks one when setting the ground rules. It’s up to each and every group to align on what the group needs while also being realistic.

## Weekly works best for new habits, monthly for maintenance

In a [large review of 20 studies on how long habits take to form](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488), the typical habit took about two months to become automatic (59 to 66 days), though individual results ranged from 4 to 335 days. In two months, a weekly group meets eight or nine times. A monthly group meets twice. Most of this research comes from health behaviors, so treat it as a strong hint rather than a rule for every kind of commitment.

The practical read is simple. If the commitment is a brand-new habit, a shorter rhythm gives you more chances to correct course while the habit is still forming. If it is maintenance, or something where a month is the natural unit of progress, such as a sales target or a savings goal, monthly holds up fine.

| Rhythm | Best for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | New habits and tight feedback loops | More overhead for busy members |
| Bi-weekly | Groups that want frequent contact without weekly pressure | Two weeks is long enough to forget a commitment mid-stretch |
| Monthly | Maintenance, month-sized goals, and genuinely busy schedules | Progress reconstructed from memory at the end |

## Record your progress as you go, not at the end

The busiest groups fail at the reporting step, not the commitment step. Waiting until the last few days of the month to reconstruct four weeks of effort from memory is where a monthly rhythm actually falls apart, and it is rarely because the commitment itself was unrealistic.

Research on tracking explains why. In a [large review of 138 experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025), people who were prompted to track their progress tracked far more, and the extra tracking helped them reach their goals more often, with the biggest gains when progress was written down and shared with others. People mostly do not track unless something makes them, and **the tracking itself is what helps.**

Recording progress means writing a note, taking a photo, or making a tally when the thing happens, not summarizing it later. It turns the review before your meeting into a five-minute job. The full method is in [keeping records that actually change behavior](/blog/how-to-run-accountability-check-ins-that-actually-change-behavior). Accountability Guild calls this logging proof, and members do it throughout the month.

### Record anything another person could look at and believe

The same things you would record for a weekly group, spread across the month and captured at the moment they happen.

| Commitment | What to record | When to record it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three workouts a week | A photo or a tally for each session | The same day |
| Finish a draft chapter | A saved version and a two-line note | At the end of each work session |
| Make prospecting calls | A tally in your notes app | After each block of calls |
| Save $500 a month | A screenshot of the transfer | The day it happens |

## Agree on what counts as an excuse before anyone needs one

Every group eventually has a month that goes sideways: travel, illness, a family emergency. If the group has not agreed in advance what counts as a legitimate excuse, that month becomes an argument about whether this one counts. A good rule names the real conflicts, and it does not cover "did not get to it." An excuse that quietly becomes a general escape hatch erodes the trust that gives any consequence its meaning.

Set the rule once, at the start, alongside the rhythm and the cost of a miss, so everyone knows how many excuses exist and what qualifies. If you are still choosing the cost, [designing consequences that build trust instead of burning it](/blog/designing-consequences-that-increase-follow-through-without-burning-trust) is worth reading first. We call this agreed excuse a “hall pass”, and a group sets the allowance up front with the rest of its ground rules.

### Allow only a few excuses a year

Treat early overuse of executes as a warning sign. Enable enough to cover a real conflict once or twice a year without it becoming a routine excuse. The exact number is a group decision, made once at the start. If the group burns through its excuses early, that is a signal the rhythm or the commitments are wrong, not that the allowance is too small.

## Structure keeps commitments alive when motivation dips

In a [study of 128 university students](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.2013-0173) who recorded their intentions daily and wore activity monitors for two weeks, habit strength predicted how active people were on the days when their motivation was lower than their own usual level. On ordinary or high-motivation days it added nothing. The researchers' framing is a useful one: a habit is a floor, not a ceiling. It does not make good days better. It stops bad days from going to zero.

A monthly group is built on the same idea. The schedule, the agreed cost of a miss, and the agreed excuse are the outside floor while the habit is still forming, and forming takes longer than a month or two. When motivation dips in month three, the structure is what keeps the commitment from going to zero. For the wider picture on how habits form and break, [behavior change](/behavior-change) covers environment design and recovering fast after a miss.

## Treat the first two months as a trial, then adjust

If monthly is not holding, because progress keeps getting reconstructed at the end or commitments keep slipping mid-month, the honest move is to agree on a shorter rhythm when you set the ground rules for the next stretch, rather than quietly tolerating a system that is not working. A rhythm is easier to change at the start of a new month than in the middle of one.

Whichever rhythm you land on, keep the summary each member shares consistent. This [weekly or four-week goal reporting template](/blog/weekly-or-4-week-goal-reporting-template-for-founders-and-operators) uses the same five blocks at either rhythm, which makes it easy to compare one stretch with the next.

## A simple pattern: set the rules, record, then review

Here is what one month can look like from start to finish:

| When | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Start of the month | The group agrees its rhythm, the cost of a miss, and its excuse rule, and each member writes their own measurable commitment |
| Through the month | Members record progress as it happens: notes, photos, and tallies |
| Last few days | A five-minute review of what you already recorded, then a short summary from each member |
| Meeting day | Misses are tallied, and the agreed cost applies unless an agreed excuse covers it |

Accountability Guild runs this pattern for a group: it calls the month a cycle, the agreed cost a forfeit, and the shared pot the forfeits go into a kitty.

## The structure remembers so you do not have to

A monthly rhythm can hold up on a busy schedule if you choose it for the right kind of commitment, record your progress as it happens, and agree on legitimate excuses before you need them. Treat the first two months as a trial, and shorten the rhythm if it is not holding. You do not need special software to start. Pick a date for your first meeting, write down one commitment each, and agree to your excuse rule before the month begins.
